Why this assessment

Simpler, more transparent, digital zone services

An investor should understand where to go, what they will receive, how long it will take, and who is responsible for the result. The assessment starts there: it documents, country by country, what is in place, what is already digital and what each country wants to improve first, so that cooperation is built on evidence.

Opening address · Investment Forum, Tajikistan · 2026

“Our goal should be to ensure that when an investor comes to Central Asia, they see a single, clear, digital and competitive investment region.”

Yerbol BukharbayevHead of the Regional Office for Central Asia, World Free Zones Organization

The next stage for the region’s zones goes beyond tax incentives and infrastructure: a digital, integrated, internationally oriented zone. An investor should be able to go from choosing a location to launching a project in a digital environment, with land and buildings, connection costs, incentives, registration and customs regimes all online. Digitalisation is not simply a technological tool; it is becoming an instrument of investment policy.

And investors no longer choose one country and one market: they look at the region as a whole. Production may take place in one country, inputs may come from another, and the logistics route may cross a third. That calls for moving from competition between individual zones towards cooperation between zones across countries, so that an investor who comes to Central Asia sees a single, clear, digital and competitive investment region.

What the assessment covers

Three questions

What exists today?

Rules, responsible institutions, procedures, and which of them are already digital.

What do you want to achieve?

The improvements the country wants first, and how urgent they are.

What is underway?

Reforms, initiatives and partners already at work, to build on and never duplicate.

After the assessment

Transparency, simplification,
digitalisation,
better regulations

A possible digital zone service could let an investor complete connected steps online, from choosing a location to launching a project. It could bring together information on available land and buildings, infrastructure and connection costs, incentives, registration and customs requirements, with verified outputs where appropriate. These are four areas of possible work, not fixed steps. Where digitalisation is chosen, the useful order is to document a procedure, simplify it, then put it online: digitising a procedure nobody has simplified only makes it faster to get wrong.

Transparency

Possible assistance could help document each procedure from the user's point of view: steps, required documents, costs, processing times, legal basis and responsible authority, kept up to date by that authority.

Simplification

Possible assistance could help identify steps and requirements that do not serve a clear purpose, and clarify which authority is responsible for each action.

Digitalisation

After procedures are simplified, possible assistance could help turn them into online services for applications, approvals, payments and renewals. UNCTAD's eRegistrations platform is one possible tool; each country decides what fits its law and capacity.

Better regulations

Possible assistance could review the zone law from two perspectives: what the country offers investors, and what it expects from them. Where the legal system allows, detailed procedures could sit in regulations rather than in the law itself, so services improve without amending the law. In practice

Working examples

These examples show what other countries have already built. They are inspiration, not a plan for Central Asia. Anything similar here would follow each country’s own rules and priorities.

Central Asia

Transparency of procedures

The Central Asia Gateway (infotradecentralasia.org) brings together the national trade portals of the five countries. For each import, export or transit procedure it shows what the user needs to know: steps, required documents, institutions and contact persons, costs and legal basis. It covers trade, not zones. This regional experience could help inform how zone procedures are documented, with adaptation to each country's priorities, institutions and legal framework.

Jamaica

Digitalisation of zone services

Jamaica's institutional set-up differs from those across Central Asia. What matters here is what the investor sees at the end: one entry point, every zone on a map, one process, one certificate.

A country can begin by answering the questionnaire. Answer it, then decide whether you want to discuss possible next steps.
Answer the questionnaire →

Completing the questionnaire creates no commitment. Subject to the partners' mandates and available resources, the responses could inform a national assessment and report. Regional findings would be presented in consolidated form; an individual country would be named only with its consent.
