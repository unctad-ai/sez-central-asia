Better zone regulations

What the country offers and what it expects

A zone law’s real work. Most laws only describe a procedure.

A zone law is not a registration procedure

A special economic zone is a bounded territory where a country adjusts its ordinary rules to welcome specific activities. That adjustment is the whole instrument: inside the zone, procedures, duties and conditions are set deliberately, for a purpose, rather than inherited from the general regime.

Yet most zone laws read as procedure: how to apply, which forms, which committee decides. The real questions sit before any procedure: what does the country offer a company that comes, what does it expect in return, and who makes sure both sides hold.

The offer

What does a company get for choosing this zone?

The questions that decide a location have shifted: how long until I am licensed, how certain is my legal position in ten years, can I bring my specialists and their families, is the power and land really there. Tax packages look much the same from one zone to the next; thirty zones compared on tax give thirty similar answers.

The ask

What does the country expect in return?

Jobs, training, technology staying local, revenue, reporting. Left vague, these stay wishes. Written as conditions in the permit or the contract, with targets that can be checked, they become enforceable.

Supervision

Who checks that both sides hold?

For a growing class of activities, advanced therapies, large-scale computing, new forms of finance, the prior question is: is there anywhere I can operate at all? They are blocked at home not for lack of merit, but because ordinary frameworks were not written for them. Competent, dedicated supervision is itself the offer.

Eight things a country can put on the table

Different activities respond to different combinations. The choice should be deliberate, not copied from what other zones offered ten years ago.

01

A home for activities that lack one

A credible place to operate for activities blocked elsewhere, with proper screening for fit and values.

02

Specialised regulation by people who understand the field

Often the most valuable element. Many activities suffer not from too much regulation but from the wrong kind. Serious investors pay for legitimacy and competent oversight, not laxity.

03

Modern administrative facilitation

Digital procedures, approval by silence after a clear deadline, templates, working languages of business.

04

Legal certainty

Independent courts or tribunals, international arbitration, quick contract enforcement.

05

Talent and family permits, bundled

Not just the founder: the team and their families, processed fast. Often the binding constraint.

06

Infrastructure that fits the activity

Labs for biotech, power and fibre for computing, testbeds for climate work. The right kind, not any kind.

07

Contractual stability under adaptive law

The framework evolves; each investor’s deal is fixed by contract for an agreed term.

08

Fiscal terms

Useful, often expected, rarely decisive. Any country can match a tax package.

What the country can get back

The familiar benefits

  • Jobs, at various skill levels depending on the activity mix.
  • Tax revenue, modest early, growing as the zone matures.
  • Investment that stays, and local suppliers pulled up the value chain.
  • Knowledge transfer, real when written into contracts and licences, not left to chance.

The forward-looking benefits

  • Position in fast-growing sectors: being the place where an activity is done well.
  • Talent attraction as a national capability, and less brain drain: local talent finds frontier work at home.
  • Regulatory reputation: being the jurisdiction whose rules others study and adopt.
  • National brand: known for one excellent thing, with a halo across exports and diplomacy.

The deepest benefit, almost never discussed

Using the zone to modernise the country’s institutions, financed by the investors who need them. Serious activities require expert regulators, capable courts, monitoring bodies. Zone revenues, fees, levies and licences can pay for them directly. Over time these become national assets: mechanisms proven inside the zone extend to the whole administration, staff trained there serve nationally, and reform happens without head-on political confrontation. The zone is small enough to try things; what works, the country keeps.

The ask becomes real through value-capture conditions: training and hiring targets, partnership requirements, technology-stay clauses, measurable deployment targets, written into contracts and licences from the start, checkable one by one.

A law drafted to evolve

Rules written for a fast-moving economy cannot lock everything for twenty years. The fix is structural: each part of the framework is revisable at its own rhythm, by its own mechanism.

Hard to change

Principles

Values, limits, fundamental commitments. The part investors and citizens rely on.

By parliament

Structure

Institutions, governance, dispute resolution.

By the authority

Operations

Activity scope, fiscal parameters, procedures. Changed with consultation and review, not a new Act.

By decision

Implementation

Forms, fees, technical standards. Versioned, and drafted so compliance can be partly automated.

Two more habits keep the framework alive: conditions instead of closed lists ("activities meeting these criteria are permitted", so a new activity can enter without reopening parliament) and review by obligation (fixed review dates, incentives that expire unless renewed with evidence). This is how respected regulators already operate: the Monetary Authority of Singapore, the FCA in the United Kingdom, the ADGM in Abu Dhabi. Adaptive is not unstable: what attracts capital is clear rules about how rules change.

The law sets the regime; the contract sets the deal. When the law evolves, existing operators continue under their contracts; new ones enter under the new framework. The framework adapts, the investor’s conditions stay stable, and the contract is what makes both possible at once.

What happens when the pieces mix

  • Minimum capital written into the text: US$ 1,500,000 for a developer. Ten years of inflation later, changing a number needs parliament.
  • Day-to-day duties on fencing, waste and site security written as statute, instead of licence conditions the authority can update.
  • A closed list of eligible industries: an activity nobody thought of when the law was written cannot enter until parliament reopens the list.
  • Concessions up to fifty years with no built-in moment to revisit them.

All four patterns from one real law: Jamaica’s Special Economic Zones Act, 2016. Each made sense to a drafter in a hurry, with no place to put it other than the law.

What this could mean for your zones

You have a law

A simplification map: what belongs at which layer, what is procedure that should move down, what is really one investor’s deal that belongs in a contract. Same protections, far less to amend.

You have none yet

The thinking before any drafting: what is the offer, what is the ask, who supervises, which layer holds each piece. The law that follows is shorter and clearer because the thinking happened first, in plain words. Local legal teams stay in charge of the drafting itself.

A model regulation

A ready structure any country can adapt, with each piece at the layer that can change it. In preparation.
